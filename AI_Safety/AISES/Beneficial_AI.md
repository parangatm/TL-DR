## Beneficial AI and Machine Ethics

### Compare Well-Being Theories

**Task**: In 150-300 words, compare two conceptions of well-being (e.g., preference satisfaction vs. objective goods). Discuss potential pitfalls if an AI is optimized for only one or the other.

**Purpose**: Clarify how philosophical definitions shape AI alignment.

Preference satisfaction theory posits well-being in terms of fulfilling individuals’ desires or choices. AI aligned with this theory will aim to give the user what they say they want. The pros are respecting personal diversity, but cons include poorly short-term preferences. The user may want things that harm them or are based on misinformation. If an AI is fine tuned solely for preference satisfaction, it could reinforce harmful addictions, and manipulative tactics, leading to long-term declines in well-being despite satisfying immediate wants.

Objective goods theory holds that certain things like health, knowledge, meaningful relationships, or autonomy are intrinsically valuable, regardless of whether someone desires them at a given moment. An AI fine tuned to this theory might promote education, health, and social connection even if the user resists them in the short term. The risk here is that the AI could override individual choices “for their own good,” potentially violating freedoms and imposing a biased vision of life.

If an AI is optimized exclusively for one theory, it risks misalignment with human values in practice. A balanced approach would integrate both in a way that it respects individual preferences while also considers objective goods, and uses transparency and adaptability to avoid narrow or overreaching interventions. 


### Designing Law-Following AI

**Task**: In 150–300 words, describe one challenge that arises when designing AI systems to follow human laws. Why might this be more difficult than it initially seems? What are some examples of edge cases or ambiguities in law that make implementation tricky?

**Purpose**: Explore the limits of a purely law-based approach to building beneficial AI and highlight the role of interpretation and social context in AI behavior.

A major challenge in designing AI systems to follow human laws is that the laws are written for human interpretation within a social context, and are often not completely objective. They contain ambiguities and exceptions require judgment based on norms and situational nuance. It is very difficult to put a clear and finite definition to commonly used terms like “reasonable force,” “undue hardship,” or “fair use”.

This challenge becomes even more pronounced in criminal proceedings, where the stakes are high and decisions rely on subtle assessments of context and intent. For example, distinguishing between self-defense and assault may require evaluating the perceived threat, the proportionality of the response, and the defendant’s state of mind. These hinge on human testimony, cultural expectations, and credibility judgments. 

Edge cases also arise when laws conflict across jurisdictions or when no clear precedent exists. For instance, cross-border data transfers under inconsistent privacy, or situations where local law is silent on emerging technologies, are difficult to quantify for AI systems. Even within one legal system, statutes may overlap or contradict.

These scenarios show why simply encoding statutes into an AI system is insufficient. Criminal law often depends not just on what happened, but how and why it happened, requiring interpretive reasoning that blends legal rules with human moral judgment.
